Research

I am fascinated by the dynamics of nonlinear systems. The dynamics of fluids such as air or water is described by nonlinear equations. These predict incredible phenomena such as rogue waves, or explain why hurricanes never split in two.
Black holes and gravitational waves are also described by nonlinear equations, the equations of General Relativity.
In my work, I focus on understanding better the nonlinear dynamics of the relaxation of black holes (BHs) to equilibrium, the dynamics of strongly gravitating fluids, and the connection between hydrodynamics and gravitational physics.

Crazy Ideas

Black holes relax to equilibrium emitting gravitational waves. This happens, for example, after two black holes merge, in the miliseconds after the remnant black hole first forms. This stage is called the ringdown.
BHs ringdown by emitting waves with particular tones. These tones only depend on the BH mass and its spin, which is incredibly useful, for example, to test General Relativity.
However the ringdown also contains higher harmonics, obtained by combining several tones. These are much weaker, but nonetheless will be detectable in the future.
The ratio between the children higher harmonic, and their parent tones, is a very interesting quantity. In 2308.14796, we computed for the first time this ratio for black holes at all spins, showing that this quantity does (mostly) not depend on the initial conditions of the merger.
The upcoming LISA mission will be able to detect these higher harmonics - so we have a novel way to test General Relativity!
Recently I have been interested in the high-frequency limit of this nonlinear behavior. We showed some interesting features in 2501.17950. Stay tuned for more results on this!
There are many more interesting aspects of black hole ringdown and nonlinear dynamics. Check our recent review on black hole spectroscopy!

Real stars are not perfect fluids. They have viscosity, which is a measure of how do they react against deformations. A very viscous material would resist deformation so much that it could be a mirror of gravitational waves, much like a conductive material can reflect light.
W. Press baptized this material Respondium. Intrigued by this idea, last year we showed that this could occur, indeed! But the viscosity would need to be so large that a first order theory of hydrodynamics would break causality.
However in the process we learned how to perturb viscous stars. They can absorb gravitational waves, and acquire new oscillation modes.
More recently we showed that if an object is viscous and rotating, it will amplify low-frequency gravitational waves. This is a phenomenon known as superradiance, but in this case without requiring event horizons or ergoregions.
This means that by studying perfect fluids we are missing out on most of the fun interactions that can occur whenever gravitational waves are emitted or propagate through astrophysical media!
How do accretion disks heat up when two black holes merge? Do they emit bright counterparts? How are gravitational waves affected by this? What are the dynamical tides of stars with large bulk viscosities? All these are questions I find exciting!

I am always on the lookout for new ideas. These are some things I find exciting!

  • Nonlinearities in the early universe: I would like to learn more about scalar induced gravitational waves, and the impliciations this has for early universe cosmology.
  • Turbulence of gravitational waves: Gravitational waves can behave turbulently if they are trapped sufficiently long. Can they be described by a kinetic wave equation? How far can the realm of wave turbulence extend to gravitational waves? Some early explorations on this can be found here.
  • How can you emit gravitational waves on the lab? How can you trap them? What happens when you focus them?
  • Higher order perturbation theory: Can we study gravitational perturbations beyond second order, in a sufficiently simple background? Do we understand gauge and frame invariance there?
  • Near-extremal black holes: perturbations only decay slowly in the near extremal limit. Do black holes ever reach zero temperature, then?
